## Hypotheses

**baseline** - The deployed configuration. Every other row is read against this one.

**dense-only** - Vector search alone loses the exact-match cases - identifiers, drug names, ICD-10 codes - that BM25 is good at.

**sparse-only** - BM25 alone loses the paraphrased cases, where the question shares no vocabulary with the document.

**hybrid-no-rerank** - The cross-encoder is the dominant cost in a request. This measures what accuracy that cost buys.

**top-k-3** - A tighter context is cheaper and less distracting, at the cost of recall.

**top-k-10** - More context should lift recall on the multi-document cases; precision must fall, since the denominator grows.

**pool-20** - A small candidate pool is faster. If the metrics hold, the deployed pool of 50 is doing nothing.

**pool-100** - A larger pool gives fusion and the reranker more to work with.

## Interpretation

**Four configurations returned byte-identical metrics, and that is the most
informative result in the table.** `baseline`, `dense-only`, `pool-20` and
`pool-100` agree to four decimal places on every retrieval metric. Four
genuinely different configurations cannot produce identical numbers by
coincidence, so the first job was to find out why rather than to read the
table.

The cause is corpus size. Chunk counts are 41 per patient plus 18 unattributed:

| scope | chunks reachable |
| --- | --- |
| one patient (the `easy`/`medium` cases) | 41 |
| the whole corpus (the `hard`, unscoped cases) | 141 |

Sixty-eight of the 107 cases are patient-scoped, and for those a candidate pool
of 50 **is the entire searchable set**. Dense, sparse and hybrid all return the
same 41 chunks in different orders; the reranker then scores the top 30 of the
fused list and the top 5 survive. When the pool already contains everything,
the strategy that filled it cannot matter, and neither can its size - which is
why `pool-20` and `pool-100` land in the same place as `pool-50`.

So the section 14 questions "vector-only vs hybrid" and "candidate pool tuning"
are **not answered by this corpus**. The measurement is sound; the corpus is too
small to put the question. `sparse-only` is the one mode that does move, and
only because BM25 returns fewer than 41 candidates for some queries (13 and 17
in the logged traces) - a genuinely smaller pool, and it scores marginally
*better* on MRR and NDCG.

**The reranker is currently making retrieval worse and costs 1.6 seconds.**
`hybrid-no-rerank` beats the deployed configuration on every retrieval metric -
recall +0.011, MRR +0.062, NDCG +0.045, precision +0.034 - while p95 falls from
1799ms to 197ms. That is a 9x latency reduction for strictly better rankings.
This is not a marginal call on the numbers as they stand.

It is, however, the regime where a cross-encoder has least to offer and most to
lose. Reranking exists to rescue a good document that fusion buried deep in a
large pool. Here there is no deep pool: the reranker is re-ordering a list that
already contains the answer near the top, so it can only hold position or make a
mistake, and `ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2` is a small model scoring clinical text it
was not trained on. The result is consistent with decision 21 in
`docs/decisions.md`, where measuring the reranker in Sprint 3 found a real bug
rather than a quality gain.

**Top-K behaves exactly as predicted, which is the one clean result here.**
Going to 3 costs recall (-0.124) and hit rate (-0.128) and buys precision
(+0.047); going to 10 saturates recall at 1.000 and halves precision. That
trade is real, it is the shape the hypothesis predicted, and it is the only
experiment in this sweep whose question the corpus is large enough to answer.

## Decision

**Keep `top_k=5`.** The 3 and 10 variants move recall and precision in opposite
directions by similar magnitudes with no latency difference, so there is no free
win; 5 sits where recall is high without handing the model twice the context to
be distracted by.

**Keep the reranker on, and record that this is a judgement made against the
evidence rather than with it.** The measurement says turn it off. The reason not
to is that the measurement was taken in the one regime where reranking cannot
help - a candidate pool equal to the corpus - and the deployed system is meant
to behave sensibly when the corpus is not 21 documents. Turning it off now would
be tuning the architecture to the size of the test fixture. `ENABLE_RERANKER` is
configuration, so this is one environment variable away from being reversed if
the trade still looks bad at scale.

That is a hypothesis about scale, not a finding, and it should be treated as one:
it is recorded in the README as a known limitation rather than as a result.

**Treat "vector-only vs hybrid" and "pool tuning" as unanswered.** Rerunning
them on this corpus would produce the same identical rows. Answering them needs
a corpus where the candidate pool is a small fraction of what is searchable -
several hundred documents, not twenty-one. Until then the deployed hybrid
configuration is a design choice (BM25 for identifiers and drug names, vectors
for paraphrase) supported by reasoning, not by these numbers.

**Next measurement worth taking:** the two experiments this sweep cannot run,
chunk size and embedding model, both change the index rather than the query and
so are not confounded by pool saturation. They are the cheapest next signal.
